# Security reviews

Dates: 2026-10-08 to 2026-10-11. This document records every internal security review of Noised: what each one looked at, what it found, how each finding was fixed and tested, and what remains. Round 1 and its follow-up 1b are §1 to §5; the later reviews are §8 to §16 (§13 collects the mutation testing, §14 is audit round 3, §15 is review 4 of the programmable launches, P1c, §16 is audit round 4); §6 is the current list of residual risks.

All of them were carried out by AI agents: finders, one per risk lens; skeptics whose job was to refute each finding; adversarial reviewers who wrote proofs of concept against the real program builds; and fixers who added a regression test for every fix. **None of this is an independent audit**, and it does not change the project's status (see the [README](../README.md)). A bare section reference such as "§4" points into this document; "SPEC §3.6" points into [SPEC.md](SPEC.md).

**Reading this document.** Finding IDs belong to their review: round 1 uses V1 to V5 for the vault, L1 to L3 for the launchpad and its hook, C1 and C2 for the clients; the clients review uses M1 to M3, L1 to L5 and I1; round 2 uses C0 to C15, in the order the review recorded them; the PQ v2 adversarial review numbers its findings 1 to 7; round 3 numbers its findings R3-1 to R3-5 and its fixes F1 to F4; review 4 numbers its proofs of concept R4-1 to R4-4 and the lead's decisions R1 to R6 (with R6a, R6b), Ra and Rb; round 4 numbers its findings by lens: L-i1 to L-i3 (launch), A4-1 to A4-4 and A4-S (autopilot), C1, C2 and M1 to M4 (custody; outside §16 written "round-4 C1", "round-4 M4" and so on) and ID-F1 to ID-F7 with ID-F2b (identity signing). They are unrelated to the guarantee IDs of [SECURITY_GUARANTEES.md](SECURITY_GUARANTEES.md) (T1, L1 to L8, V1 to V6, A1 to A7, M1 to M4, CR1 to CR5, AP1 to AP7), which this document always writes as "guarantee L1", "guarantee V5" and so on, and to the residual IDs R1 to R41 of [THREAT_MODEL.md](THREAT_MODEL.md), which it writes as "THREAT_MODEL R21".

## Summary

- **Programs.** Every confirmed finding of rounds 1, 1b, 2 and 3 and of the PQ v2 and metadata reviews against the four programs is fixed and has a regression test that replays the attack against the mainnet program binaries in LiteSVM, except round 1's V5 (a documented limitation), round 2's C7 and C8 (write-lock contention, which the longer windows make linearly more expensive but no on-chain rule can remove), and round 3's R3-2, R3-3 and R3-5 (documented residuals and a client-side check, §14). Round 3's two program fixes are merged: F1 (an attestation whose window passed unused is replaceable, and a window needs 60 s left when its attestation lands) and F2 (while the guard is on, transfers other than sells only into token accounts whose owner can never change). On the merged programs (2026-10-10, [verification summary](evidence/2026-10-10-audit3-final-verification.md), overall PASS) every suite passes: host 157, the LM-OTS/LMS known-answer tests 35, `tests/integration` 103, the launchpad's LiteSVM suite 69 and the shield's 18, plus three independent Python reference checks; the integration mutation run kills 170 of 170 mutants, the LiteSVM-only run 50 of 50 (5 equivalent mutants survive, as documented) and the host run of the new token-account reader 8 of 8 (§13, SPEC §6).
- **Programmable launches (P1c) and review 4.** The creator's launch rules (PXR1, enforced by the hook, bound into attestation v3 and certificate v4), `pq_authorize` and the autopilot program were merged on 2026-10-11, then reviewed (§15). Every program decision of review 4 is merged with regression tests and mutants: holders' exits through the PQ vault and the Shield never meet creator rules (R1), sells and everything after the guard are decided from the policy's fixed head (R3), `pq_authorize` writes only the three kinds the autopilot reads (Ra), the rules-only reads are lazy (Rb), `launch` re-hashes the rules record (R6a), the autopilot reads only the launchpad versions it knows (R6b), and a freeze authorization expires (R5). Insider-only buying and vacuous rules stay possible and are disclosed (R2). The final run on the merged tree (2026-10-11, [verification summary](evidence/2026-10-11-review4-final-verification.md), overall PASS) passes host 317, the rules crate 81, known answers 35, `tests/integration` 113, the launchpad's LiteSVM suite 82, the shield's 19 and the autopilot's 27, plus four Python reference checks; its mutation runs kill every killable mutant (§13, §15).
- **Audit round 4** (§16) found no exploitable issue in the launch path and none in the vault, the Shield or `noised-lmots`. Its program fixes are merged with regression tests and mutants: a keeper's partial buyback no longer uses up a node's run (A4-2), a revocation names the activation it revokes so a re-appointed operator can be revoked again (A4-3, with the running graph's activation stored, A4-S), burns earn no tip before graduation (A4-4), a distribution skips recipients without a wSOL account so keepers never pay their rent (M4), and `commit` refuses after the batch (L-i2). The final run on the merged tree (2026-10-11, [verification summary](evidence/2026-10-11-audit4-final-verification.md), overall PASS) passes host 323, the rules crate 81, known answers 35, `tests/integration` 113, the launchpad's LiteSVM suite 84, the shield's 19 and the autopilot's 36, plus four Python reference checks, and its mutation runs kill the autopilot's whole table, 234 of 234. The round's most serious finding is client-side: a dishonest RPC could steer a second device onto the leaf of a signature it withheld (ID-F1, medium-high). That fix is **done** in SDK 0.9.0 and in the app: the leaf choice is secret and keyed, signing stops with `UnknownLeavesError` when the chain shows used leaves this device does not know (the app's "key used elsewhere" flow: import the other device's records, or confirm in writing a small count after a re-check), signed times are checked against this device's clock (`ClockSkewError`), "pending check, reserve, sign" runs under a cross-tab signing lock, and unlanded signatures are capped (`SignatureCapError`). The other client fixes are done too, in SDK 0.9.0, `@noisedlabs/mcp` 0.2.0 and the app.
- **No finding was ever confirmed against `noised_shield`'s own code** (round 3's R3-5 concerns how the hook's max-wallet rule treats the Shield escrow), and none against the LM-OTS/LMS verification in `noised-lmots` itself (round 2's C15 concerned the crate's NIST wording, now fixed).
- **Clients.** Every SDK finding is fixed in SDK 0.3 (24 test files, 467 tests). SDK 0.4.0 (unreleased, in `sdk/`) carries the on-chain metadata support and round 3's client changes: the flow status after an unused window, the 60-second window rule, the `MutableOwner` error and destination checks, and the fee-recipient refusal ([sdk/CHANGELOG.md](../sdk/CHANGELOG.md)). SDK 0.5.0 (unreleased, in `sdk/`) adds the QSL-1 checker (`checkQsl1`, `qslBadge`) and the guard against older unlanded attestation signatures (round 3 fix review, info #2: `PendingAttestationError`, `landOrBurnPending`); its own verification passed (typecheck, 770 tests in 30 files, build; mutants in §13), and no review of it is recorded here yet. SDK 0.6.0 and 0.7.0 (unreleased) add the programmable module, the autopilot builders and keepers, attestation v3, certificate v4 with the masked `(checks & 4095) === 4095` test, `pq_authorize` and the quick launch; SDK 0.8.0 (unreleased) applies review 4's client decisions (R2's levels and warnings, R5's freeze payload v2, R6's simulator). SDK 0.9.0 carries audit round 4's client fixes: the keyed leaf choice and the refusals of ID-F1, the clock check, the signature caps, the per-launch lock, the import rules, the keeper's own observation before buybacks, revocation payload v3 and the new autopilot fields, and the graph-recipient check (§16); `@noisedlabs/mcp` 0.2.0 carries the MCP server's fixes for untrusted text. SDK 0.4.0 to 0.9.0 were never published one by one: 0.9.0 is the first npm release and contains all of them. The app has adopted SDK 0.9.0 (`app/test/sdk09-adoption.test.ts`); the app-side items of the clients review and of round 2 are listed in §6 item 16.
- **Residuals** that no fix removes are listed in §6: write-lock contention in the commit, execution and launch windows, the first transaction in a launch window winning, relayers delaying signed transactions, an older unlanded attestation signature taking a freed slot, two devices that do not share their one-time-key ledgers, a vault's batch quota used up by others' commits, shared accounts filled to max-wallet during the guard, control of a position changing without a token transfer (shared wallets, PDA and smart-wallet owners, PQ vault key rotation, delegates), and the third parties Noised cannot remove. Since P1c also: creator rules that restrict buys to listed wallets for up to the guard, vacuous rules that still carry `RULES_BOUND`, creator rules (never the built-in checks) passed by routing through the vault or the Shield, the autopilot's reference-price and liveness residuals, withheld authorizations, and the operator and keepers being classical keys (§6 items 24 to 30). Since round 4 also: the buyback bound's dependence on an honest observation in each window, revocations bound to one activation, distributions that skip recipients without an account, signing clients' dependence on the device's own clock, and price conditions limited to `u64` (§6 items 27 and 31 to 33).

## 1. Method

### Finders

Seven finders worked in parallel, each with one lens:

| Lens | What it covered |
|---|---|
| Accounts and PDAs | Signer, owner and address checks; PDA seeds, bumps and seed collisions; squatting through `init` and `init_if_needed`; `has_one` bindings; canonical ATAs; token-program and mint confusion; duplicate mutable accounts; Anchor 1.x pitfalls; account close and revival. |
| CPI and Meteora integration | Every CPI into DBC, DAMM v2, Token-2022 and the hook: program-ID checks, signer-seed scope, forwarding of `remaining_accounts`, CPI depth, stale data after a CPI. Also the DBC config the launchpad builds against DBC's own validation, and whether `execute_batch`, `claim_fees` and `claim_lp_fees` can be pointed at another pool or position. |
| Token-2022 and the transfer hook | Ways to bypass Launch Guard. Ways for it to block a sell, a fee claim, the completing swap or migration (a honeypot). Abuse of exempt owners, meta-list resolution by Meteora's SDK, extension parsing, and transfer-fee, frozen, closed, memo-required and CPI-guarded accounts. |
| Economics | Commit caps and windows, partial fills, pro-rata claims, refunds, expiry, dust, donations, fee routing, the shield's backing invariant, integer math, and any way for the creator to extract value: self-sniping, fee capture, front-running. |
| Post-quantum crypto and the vault state machine | `noised-lmots` against RFC 8554 §4 and its TypeScript twin; what the withdrawal message binds; one-time-key hazards (any revealed signature that cannot execute); key-id and PDA collisions; the successor and sweep rules; compute budget; SDK key derivation. |
| Liveness and DoS | Anything that can stall or brick a launch, a batch, fee distribution, a vault or the shield: compute and size limits, rent, pre-created accounts, accounts a recipient controls, time windows, Meteora-side liveness. |
| Policy completeness | Each SPEC §7 invariant and each fixed DBC value of SPEC §1.2 traced to the code. The launch's checks on the new mint, the certificate, `initialize_protocol`. Any path that changes a launch after creation. Whether the SDK verifier checks live state or trusts the certificate. |

Every agent read SPEC.md, [research/FACTS.md](research/FACTS.md), the code, and the Meteora, Anchor 1.2.1 and Token-2022 sources. None could modify the repository. To confirm a scenario, agents wrote proof-of-concept tests in their own scratch copies, outside the repository. These ran in LiteSVM 0.17 against the mainnet dumps of DBC, DAMM v2, Token-2022, SPL Token (p-token) and the ATA program, the fixtures that `scripts/fetch-fixtures.{ps1,sh}` records with their SHA-256.

Severity scale given to every agent:

- critical: direct loss, theft or permanent lock of user funds, or a broken core guarantee;
- high: conditional loss, or a bypass of a guarantee;
- medium: griefing, denial of service, liveness, or wrong accounting without direct theft;
- low: defense in depth.

The agents were also given five design points the project had already accepted. They were to re-report one only with a concrete exploit beyond it:

1. During the snipe shield, anyone may buy into the exempt batch escrow or the launch authority's ATA. This is a donation at the buyer's own cost.
2. The execution window is 30 seconds.
3. Meteora's programs and Token-2022 are upgradeable.
4. A vault withdrawal binds the recipient wallet and pays its canonical ATA (`init_if_needed`). `open_vault` refuses PermanentDelegate mints and transfer-fee mints with a live authority.
5. Batch claims are exempt from the hook's caps.

Two confirmed findings went beyond them: V1 defeats point 4, and L2 defeats point 1.

### Skeptics

Reports that named the same file, a line within 3 of each other, and the same first three words of the title were merged automatically. That left 24 findings. Each went to independent skeptics, who were told to refute it from the code paths and the Meteora, Token-2022 and Anchor sources, and to answer "not real" unless the scenario held. A finding reported as critical or high got three skeptics, a medium one got two, and a low one got one. A finding was confirmed when more than half of its skeptics found it real. Many skeptics wrote their own proofs of concept against the same mainnet binaries. Several corrected details of the original report, such as forgery costs, transaction sizes or the set of affected mints.

That makes 7 finders and 45 skeptics: 52 agents. All 41 verdicts on the 20 confirmed findings said "real". Each rejected finding was refuted by its skeptic (§5). The 20 confirmed reports were then merged by hand into 10 issues. V1 alone was reported 6 times by 5 lenses, and L1 by 3 lenses. Where skeptics disagreed on severity, the table in §3 uses the majority for the merged issue.

## 2. How the fixes were checked

The fixes changed the programs: vault v4, the hook's shield rule, the pro-rata batch, the claim accounts. Agents then ported the test suites to the new interfaces. They turned the auditors' proof-of-concept set-ups into regression tests named after each finding. They found no bug in the fixes and changed no program file.

The LiteSVM and mutation results in this table were measured on the round-1 fixes, before round 1b; the two host-test rows were re-run after the round-1b code change. §4 gives every suite's results on the round-1b code.

| Suite | Result on 2026-10-08 |
|---|---|
| `tests/integration`: vault and hook, LiteSVM 0.17 against mainnet Token-2022, p-token and ATA, with SPL Memo as bundled by LiteSVM | 56 tests pass: harness 14, hook 12, SDK vectors 1, vault 29. |
| `programs/noised-launchpad/tests/litesvm`: against mainnet DBC, DAMM v2, Token-2022, SPL Token and ATA | 11 tests pass (audit regressions 6, lifecycle 1, smoke 4), in three runs. |
| `programs/noised-shield/tests/litesvm` | 13 tests pass. |
| `tests/integration/mutants.ps1`: deliberately broken copies of the vault and the hook | 49 of 49 mutants killed. They include mutants of the new code: exempt recipients reopening the shield; a batch check that ignores the program ID, ignores the discriminator, or reads the wrong instruction; every shield buy counted as the batch buy; no memo before paying a memo-required account; `release` paying any account, or not creating a missing ATA. |
| Host unit tests after the round-1b change: `cargo test -p noised-hook -p noised-vault -p noised-launchpad` | 59 tests pass: hook 12, vault 1, launchpad 46. |
| `cargo test -p noised-lmots --features signer,sha2-backend` | 12 tests pass, including RFC 8554 Appendix F test case 2 and the deterministic randomizer of C1. |

Two adversarial reviewers then checked the work, one for the vault and the hook and one for the launchpad and the shield. Each read every regression test in its area to check that it runs the real attack, not a weakened one, and asserts exact error codes and balances. They also looked for new bugs in the fixed programs. They edited no file.

- **Vault and hook: verdict "sound".** The reviewer rebuilt both programs; the builds were byte-identical to the tested binaries. It re-ran the 56 tests and the 49 mutants, then wrote 7 more mutants, all killed:
  - the ATA owner-field check added back (caught by `v1a_…`);
  - the memo sent after the transfer instead of before (`v1b_…`);
  - the payout's rent recorded for the recipient instead of the payer;
  - the payout closed to whoever releases it;
  - the shield ending one second late;
  - `execute_batch` accepted anywhere in the transaction;
  - the next key compared with the genesis key instead of the current key.

  Its probes passed:
  - a memo-required recipient on a hooked mint (the memo and the transfer run at depth 2, the hook at depth 3);
  - a recipient ATA address pre-funded with lamports;
  - a reassigned ATA closed by its new owner (`release` re-creates it for the wallet);
  - a rent refund to a payer that emptied its account.

  It found no new bug. It did find one documentation claim that did not hold (§4).
- **Launchpad and shield: verdict "gaps".** The reviewer re-ran both suites (11 and 13 tests pass) and probed four cases on the round-1 code:
  - A buy whose output rounds to zero passed the shield, and on a near-empty first curve segment it re-created L2. This is the new finding of §4.
  - A buy whose output account is the base vault itself was refused, but only by DBC's duplicate-account check (error 2040). §4 covers it too.
  - A claim that omits only the owner's base-token ATA fails with `AccountMismatch`.
  - On the default curve, a zero-output buy changed neither the price nor the volatility fee; the batch then paid the floor fee plus that buy's 1-lamport fee.

  It also found three regression tests that prove less than they claim (§4). Otherwise it judged the claim, commit and `execute_batch` changes sound: payout bounds, rounding, ATA creation order and duplicate-account handling.

### Details

**V1.** Six reports from five lenses. All 17 skeptic verdicts confirmed it, most of them by running the attack against the mainnet binaries:

- **SPL Token (p-token).** After the recipient ran `SetAuthority(AccountOwner)` on its ATA, every relay failed with `ConstraintTokenOwner` (2015). Passing another account of the recipient failed with `AccountNotAssociatedTokenAccount` (3014). If the ATA is handed to a key nobody holds, it can never be closed or re-created, so the lock is permanent.
- **Token-2022, memos.** With required memos enabled, the vault's transfer failed with `NoMemo` (custom error 36). A memo that the relayer places first in the transaction does not count. Token-2022 looks for the memo in the instruction just before the transfer, at the transfer's own CPI level, and only the vault can put one there.
- **Token-2022, confidential transfers.** On a confidential-transfer mint, a recipient that had turned off non-confidential credits caused `NonConfidentialTransfersDisabled` (49).
- **No malice needed.** An exchange deposit account that already requires memos blocked the withdrawal in the same way.
- **When.** The recipient could set the trap before the owner signed, because no client looked at the recipient's account.
- **Cost of the only exit.** The only way out was a second signature with the same one-time key. The skeptics estimated what forgery costs once two W4 signatures exist, by exact computation or by sampling. About 10% of signature pairs need at most about 2^36 SHA-256 evaluations; the medians they found range from about 2^54 to 2^61.

v4 implements the design the crypto lens proposed: a reserved payout, permissionless delivery with a memo call, and a delivery path signed by the recipient. Several finders and skeptics proposed an alternative, accepting any token account whose owner field is the recipient; it was not adopted. A recipient can now delay only its own payout. The withdrawal, the rest of the vault and every other payout go ahead (SPEC §3.6, §3.7). Not comparing the owner field has a cost: §6, item 1.

**V2.** Three reports. The mistake is the signer's own: naming its own vault or its successor, for example to "move everything to my next vault". A skeptic reproduced error 2040 on both token programs, for several amounts including 0, and whether or not the successor existed yet. In v4, a withdrawal that names another vault is a deposit into that vault.

**V3.** Proof of concept against mainnet Token-2022: a 1% fee (no maximum), five withdrawals of 1,000 from a vault holding 1,000,000. The recipient received 4,950, the chain of vaults kept 946,136, and 48,914 (about 4.9%) was withheld for good. A pure key rotation (`max_amount` = 0) cost 1% of the whole balance. v4 is the alternative fix the auditors named: a stable vault with the key rotated in place. In the regression test, five rotations move nothing. Each payout of X lowers the vault by exactly X, and the recipient receives X minus the fee on X.

**V4.** The proof of concept closed the empty mint and re-created it at the same address with a permanent delegate. It then minted into the vault's token account, and the delegate moved everything out. The attack needs both the close-authority key and the mint's keypair: the issuer has them, and so would a quantum attacker who recovers both Ed25519 keys. Supply must be zero to close a mint, so only deposits made after the swap are exposed. The refusal also keeps `withdraw` live, because an eligible mint can never be closed (SPEC §3.6). The regression test replays the attack and shows that the re-created mint is refused too. It also shows that once a close authority is None, Token-2022 refuses both setting a new one and closing the mint.

**V5.** No attacker gains anything; the loss comes from the sender's own mistake. A "vault address" is the natural thing to copy, though, which makes the mistake likely. The skeptic noted that the proposed on-chain fix has a problem of its own. Paying the vault's spare lamports to the recipient inside `withdraw` fails the runtime's rent check when the recipient holds no SOL and the amount is below the rent-exempt minimum. Anyone could cause that with a dust donation to the vault PDA, which would break the rule that a signed withdrawal always executes. Recovering other mints would also have to be part of the single one-time signature. The regression test pins the current behaviour: a withdrawal that names the other mint fails (`ConstraintSeeds`), and the stray SOL and tokens stay where they are.

**L1.** Three lenses reported it.

- **Economics lens, against mainnet DBC.** `[launch, commit(10 SOL)]` fit in one 1,112-byte legacy transaction (180,866 CU). Every later commit, even of 1 lamport, failed with `CapExceeded`. The creator then claimed 100% of the batch: 75.31% of the supply, against a 1% max-wallet.
- **Policy lens.** The same attack with a 1 SOL cap on a 10 SOL threshold gave the creator 26.76% of the supply.

The fix is the one the auditors proposed. In the regression test, the creator again sends `[launch, commit(cap)]` in one transaction (1,191 bytes):

- others can still commit until the last second of the window;
- the per-wallet cap still refuses commits over it;
- with 6,000,000,002 lamports committed against a 2 SOL cap, the batch spends exactly the cap;
- every committer, the creator included, gets exactly its pro-rata tokens and refund;
- the creator ends with a third of the batch instead of all of it.

Two further suggestions were not adopted: a minimum `batch_total_cap`, and refusing commits in the launch slot. Skeptics noted the second would only delay the race by a second.

**L2.** Proof of concept against mainnet DBC, with a 1% floor fee and a dynamic fee whose maximum, about 8.9%, a 0.4% move of the square-root price reaches:

- A 0.0105 SOL buy into the launch authority's ATA, 12 seconds after launch, raised the partner fees on a 4 SOL batch from 32,000,000 to 316,616,980 lamports.
- The batch received 4.24% fewer tokens.
- After paying for the buy, the creator's recipients came out 0.217 SOL ahead, about 5.4% of the batch.
- The decay settings made no difference: DBC never lowers the accumulator before it prices a swap.

All three skeptics confirmed it; one rated it medium, because the fee stays within the launch's own on-chain ceiling. The auditors proposed bounding the dynamic fee in `policy.rs`. They warned against a minimum-output or zero-accumulator check in `execute_batch`, because one cheap buy would then force every batch to expire. The fix takes a different route and removes the pre-batch buy itself. The fee bounds are unchanged: floor plus maximum dynamic fee at most 10% (guarantee F5). The fix also withdraws accepted design point 1: buys into exempt accounts during the shield are refused instead of tolerated as donations. [HOOKS.md](HOOKS.md) §2 explains why instruction introspection identifies the batch reliably: CPIs never appear as top-level instructions.

The regression test uses the proof of concept's fee settings and tries to buy into four accounts: the batch escrow, the launch authority's ATA, and an account the attacker opened for each of the two PDAs. It tries 12 seconds after launch, at `commit_end_ts`, and in the same transaction as a real `execute_batch`. Every attempt fails with `SnipeShield`, and Token-2022 refuses any other account put in the sysvar's place. Afterwards:

- the pool account is byte-for-byte unchanged;
- the batch pays exactly the floor fee and receives as many tokens as on a launch with no dynamic fee;
- the first public trade after the shield pays the floor fee plus the variable fee the batch itself left.

These assertions hold for the buys the test makes. It never tries a buy that receives zero tokens, which is how the post-fix review got through; round 1b added that case as `l2b_zero_output_buys_cannot_cross_the_snipe_shield` (§4).

**L3.** Two reports, both low. The proof of concept used an expired batch. After the committer reassigned its wSOL ATA, `claim` failed with error 2015, and with `AccountMismatch` when the ATA was left out. In v3 an executed batch usually refunded nothing, so only expired or partly filled batches were affected; since the L1 fix, every oversubscribed batch refunds.

The skeptics confirmed the mechanism but argued against this fix. `claim` is permissionless, so it now pays a reassigned wSOL ATA wherever its owner field points, even to a drainer who tricked the committer into reassigning it. They suggested a claim signed by the owner to any account it chooses, or separate claims for tokens and refund. The project kept the rule `distribute` uses, so that nobody but the committer can block a claim; the trade-off is §6, item 1.

The regression tests, in order:

- in an oversubscribed batch, `claim` pays a reassigned wSOL ATA, leaving its owner field untouched, and creates a missing base-token ATA for the committer;
- any non-canonical account in either slot is refused;
- an expired batch refunds in full, both to a reassigned wSOL ATA and to a closed one, which `claim` re-creates.

**C1.** Found by the crypto lens in `app/` and `sdk/`. It computed the forgery cost exactly over 2,000 random signature pairs. Two signatures under one key let an attacker forge a message of its choice with at most:

| Share of signature pairs | SHA-256 evaluations |
|---|---|
| 1% | 2^31.7 |
| 10% | 2^36.4 |
| 25% | 2^41.2 |
| median | 2^60.8 |

With three signatures the median falls to 2^30.2. The app already refused to sign again while it held a record for the vault, and the SDK refused within one process. A second signature therefore needed a lost record, a second device or a restart. The documents now state the rule: [POST_QUANTUM.md](POST_QUANTUM.md) §6 (then §5), rules 1 and 4, and guarantee V2. The crate's derivation, `C = HMAC-SHA256(SEED, "phalanx/pq/lmots/C" ‖ I ‖ u32be(q) ‖ msg)`, is modelled on SLH-DSA's `PRF_msg` (FIPS 205): an HMAC keyed by a secret, over the message. Round 1 first documented a different form, `SHA256("phalanx/pq/lmots/C" ‖ SEED ‖ I ‖ u32be(q) ‖ msg)`, and the test client used it. Round 1b withdrew that form everywhere, and no signer may use it: two signers using different forms would again turn one withdrawal into two signatures. Status after round 1b: §4.

**C2.** Found by the crypto lens. The location it named had already changed during the review. The skeptic narrowed the impact:

- A payout to an ATA address lands in a nested ATA. The wallet can probably recover it with the ATA program's `RecoverNested`. The skeptic read this from the ATA program without testing it, and doubted it for a mint with an active hook.
- A payout to a token account that is not an ATA, or to a mint address, is lost.

The case that mattered for the vault, an unexecutable signature (V2), is gone in v4. What remains is a lost payout to an address that cannot sign.

## 4. Round 1b: found after the fixes

Round 1b is the follow-up to the review of the round-1 fixes (§2), on the same day. The launchpad reviewer found one more way through the snipe shield; the lead fixed it in the hook and the launchpad. Test agents then wrote the missing regression tests, ported the reviewers' probes, and re-ran every suite on the fixed code. They found no bug in the round-1b code and changed no program file.

### The zero-output buy: a residual of L2 (high)

The launchpad reviewer rated it high: it re-opened L2 with the same impact, through creator-chosen parameters that both `policy.rs` and DBC accept. The round-1 documents had listed the case as an untested risk limited to unusual curves (THREAT_MODEL R16), mitigated by an SDK verifier check of near-empty curve segments that did not exist.

**Root cause.** Two facts combined:

- **The round-1 hook passed every zero-amount transfer before its shield check**, in `execute` (an early return for a destination equal to the base vault or a zero amount) and in `rules::evaluate`. A zero-amount transfer moves no tokens, so this looked harmless.
- **DBC 0.2.1 executes a buy whose output rounds down to zero.** `process_swap` requires only a non-zero input, and an exact-in swap requires only `output >= minimum_amount_out`, which a minimum of 0 always meets. The input pays the fee, the rest still moves the square-root price and the volatility accumulator, and DBC sets the pool's `has_swap` flag. The base transfer goes out with amount 0, and Token-2022 calls the hook for it. Only the transfer is empty, not the swap.

**The reviewer's probes**, against mainnet DBC in a scratch copy:

- **Default test curve.** A 1-lamport buy during the shield went through. It paid a 1-lamport fee and set the pool's `has_swap` flag, but moved neither the price nor the volatility accumulator, and the batch then paid the floor fee.
- **Near-empty first segment.** The reviewer used a curve whose first segment holds liquidity 1 over 2% of the square-root price, with the dynamic fee of the L2 proof of concept. A 2-lamport buy 12 seconds after launch received 0 tokens, moved the square-root price by 2% and set the accumulator to its maximum. The 4 SOL batch then paid 395,666,224 lamports in fees instead of 40,000,000: L2 again. 80% of the extra is partner fee, which `distribute` pays to the treasury and the creator's fee recipients.
- **Output into the base vault.** A buy whose output account is the base vault itself was refused, but only by DBC's duplicate-account check (error 2040). The round-1 hook would have read such a transfer as a sell, because its destination is the base vault.

**The fix: two layers.**

1. **The hook refuses every buy until the shield ends, whatever its amount.** A buy is any transfer out of the base vault. Until `snipe_shield_end_ts` the hook refuses it unless the transaction's current top-level instruction is the launchpad's `execute_batch`, and it checks this before the zero-amount pass and before exemptions. A transfer out of the base vault is never treated as a sell, even when its destination is the base vault. Sells, and zero-amount transfers that are not buys, still always pass.
2. **The batch must be the pool's first swap.** `execute_batch` requires the DBC pool's `has_swap == 0` (`checks::dbc_pool_has_swapped`) and otherwise fails with `PoolAlreadyTraded`, appended at the end of `LaunchpadError` (code 6038) so that no existing code changes. If anything ever gets past the shield again, the batch can only expire and refund every commit in full; it can no longer buy at a moved price or fee. The auditors had warned against a minimum-output or zero-accumulator check in `execute_batch` as the fix for L2, because one cheap buy would then force every batch to expire. Here the first layer refuses every earlier buy, so this check fires only if that layer fails.

**The hook's rule for a buy into the base vault is defense in depth only.** Both round-1b test agents found that the hook never sees that transfer: Token-2022 v11 returns from a transfer whose source and destination are the same account before it moves funds or calls the hook. What stops such a buy is DBC's `ConstraintDuplicateMutableAccount` check on `base_vault` (error 2040), with the first-swap check behind it. Tests pin both facts, so a change in either program shows up.

**Residual.** The first layer depends on DBC making a Token-2022 transfer, which the hook sees, for every buy, and on DBC refusing the base vault as a buy's output account. A DBC release that changed either would let such a buy run unseen; the second layer then turns the batch into a full refund. That loses the fair open but costs committers nothing (THREAT_MODEL R16).

**Tests.** All pass on the round-1b code. Run against the round-1 builds, `l2b_…` and `l2c_…` fail, as they should; the other new launchpad tests pin documented or third-party behaviour and pass on both.

### Regression tests that proved less than they claimed

### Other round-1b findings

- **A long curve did not fit a legacy `create_launch`.** With 16 curve points `create_launch` is 1,315 bytes, over the 1,232-byte legacy limit, while the on-chain policy accepted 16 points. Fixed: the policy now caps curves at 8 points (`MAX_CURVE_POINTS`), so the largest accepted `create_launch` fits a legacy transaction (SPEC §1.2).
- **Measured costs.** `execute_batch` took at most 161,289 CU (16-point curve), of which the hook used 5,521. A v4 `withdraw` takes about 79k–106k CU in a 2,693-byte v1 transaction; with `release` the transaction is at most 2,977 bytes. SPEC §3.9, HOOKS.md §7 and POST_QUANTUM.md §6 carry the full numbers.
- **Two equivalent hook mutants.** Dropping `is_buy` or `now < snipe_shield_end_ts` from the `in_batch` computation, or dropping `amount == 0` from `execute`'s early return for transfers that are not buys, changes only which accounts are read, and so the compute cost, never a decision. They are documented, not added.
- **Empty payouts.** `PayoutReleased` for an empty payout delivered by `release` names whatever account the caller passed as `destination`, which the program ignores. The amount is 0, so indexers should key on the amount. Informational.

## 6. Residual risks (current, 2026-10-11)

What remains after every review below. Each item says where it is documented for users.

1. **Payouts and claims follow a reassigned ATA.** The vault's `release` and the launchpad's `claim` and `distribute` pay the recipient's canonical ATA without comparing its owner field.
   - An SPL Token ATA (wSOL, USDC and so on) whose owner field was reassigned is paid wherever it now points, and anyone can trigger that payment.
   - A wallet tricked into signing `SetAuthority(AccountOwner)` by an owner-change drainer therefore loses later payouts and refunds to the new owner.
   - The skeptics of L3 argued against this trade-off. The project accepted it so that nobody but the holder can block a payout. A committer can avoid it by naming a PQ vault as its refund owner: a vault never reassigns its token account.
   - The tokens of a claim reach a Token-2022 ATA, which cannot be reassigned.
   - A vault recipient can have its payout delivered elsewhere with `release_to`, but cannot stop someone else from calling `release` first.
2. **One-time keys depend on the client keeping its ledger.** The programs make a revealed signature always executable (vault) or always landable (identity), and accept each one-time key at most once on-chain. Whether a key ever signs two messages is decided off-chain. The SDK enforces it with durable ledgers (an atomic `add` before any signature, the signature recorded before it is returned), in-process guards, deterministic randomizers and authenticated export and import between devices. What remains:
   - two processes or devices with two ledgers that were not synced (or a custom ledger without an atomic `add`, or one that answers "reserved" to every caller) can each sign once: for LMS leaves the collision odds are about 1 in 1,024 per pair of messages, for a vault key it needs the same sequence on both. **Audit round 4 (ID-F1, medium-high) showed those odds held only against an honest RPC:** the SDK's leaf probe was public and skipped every leaf the RPC reported as used, so an RPC that withheld one device's signature could report fake bits and steer the other device onto that leaf with certainty. The fix (done in SDK 0.9.0 and the app) makes the probe secret and keyed, lets no on-chain bit move it, and refuses to sign when the chain shows a used leaf the device has no record of, until that device's records are imported (`UnknownLeavesError`; the app offers the import, or a typed confirmation for at most 3 unknown signatures after reading the key's record again). Best practice for users: one device per quantum key unless you import its records (§16);
   - the in-process guards reset on reload; they complement the ledgers, they do not replace them;
   - keys an import reports as `unverifiable` are not recorded on the importing device; the caller must refuse to sign until the other device completes and re-exports them (the SDK's example stops);
   - a client that is not the SDK must reproduce the same rules (SPEC §3.10, POST_QUANTUM.md §3.5 and §6).
3. **SOL or other mints sent to a vault PDA are lost (V5).** Deposit only into the vault's token account. For wSOL, transfer SOL there and call `SyncNative`, which anyone may call (SPEC §3.8).
4. **A payout can be delayed, and it cannot be cancelled.**
   - A freeze or pause authority, a transfer hook (for example Launch Guard's wallet-to-wallet lock during a guard window), or a recipient account that cannot receive delays a payout. The payout stays reserved, and the rest of the vault is unaffected.
   - A recipient whose canonical ATA cannot receive can use `release_to`. A PDA recipient can use it only if its program signs.
   - Once `withdraw` executes, the reserved amount belongs to the named wallet. A wrong recipient loses that payout, never the vault; `checkRecipient` and the confirmation the signers require exist to prevent that.
   - The vault decides whether to send a memo by reading the destination's extensions. An account extension unknown to its Token-2022 interface crate, which only a future Token-2022 upgrade could introduce, makes it assume no memo is needed. `release` to a memo-requiring account with such an extension would fail, but `release_to` still works.
5. **Launch Guard relies on `execute_batch` and on DBC.**
   - During the shield, the hook admits a buy whenever the current top-level instruction is the launchpad's `execute_batch`, whichever launch it belongs to. That is safe today: `execute_batch` makes exactly one DBC swap, on the pool and mints bound to its own launch, with exactly the four validated hook accounts, and runs no code an attacker controls. It then ends only its own launch's shield (`end_snipe_shield`, signed by the launchpad's hook-authority PDA, only earlier). Any future change to `execute_batch` must keep these properties; until the launchpad is finalized, its upgrade authority could change it.
   - The hook sees a buy only as the Token-2022 transfer DBC makes for it. DBC 0.2.1 makes one for every buy, even one that pays out nothing, and refuses a buy into the base vault itself. A DBC release that changed either would let a buy run unseen before the batch; the first-swap check then turns the batch into a full refund (THREAT_MODEL R16). `l2b_…` and `l2d_…` fail if DBC's behaviour changes, so re-running the launchpad suite on fresh dumps after each DBC upgrade catches it.
   - A buy that completes the curve is seen too only because of DBC's order inside that swap: DBC 0.2.1 makes the output transfer, which runs the hook (`process_swap.rs` lines 302 to 310), before it revokes the hook (around lines 366 and 370). A release that revoked first would let a curve-completing buy pass the snipe shield unchecked. `l_i1_a_buy_completing_the_curve_during_the_shield_is_refused` (audit round 4, L-i1) fails if the order changes.
6. **The hook's extra accounts must never change in place.** Round 1 changed the hook's meta list from one extra account to two. A mint's meta list is written once and has no update instruction. A hook program upgraded in place over mints whose lists hold another layout would make every transfer of those mints fail, sells included. Nothing was deployed when the list changed, and both devnet (2026-10-12) and mainnet (2026-10-13) were deployed fresh with the two-entry list. [OPERATIONS.md](OPERATIONS.md#deployment-invariants) states it as a deployment invariant, together with finalizing all five programs after verification.
7. **The batch is pro rata, so capital still counts.** A participant with more capital, the creator included, gets a proportionally larger share; `batch_wallet_cap` applies per wallet, so many wallets get around it. Since round 2 the caps have floors (a total cap of at least a tenth of the threshold, a wallet cap of at least the total cap / 200). Batch claims remain exempt from the hook's caps; what one PQ vault receives is held to one wallet's cap, but a committer's own wallet receives only its own capped share.
8. **A committer can still block its own claim.** `claim` pays tokens and refund in one instruction and sends no memo. A committer whose Token-2022 base-token ATA requires memos, or cannot receive for another reason, blocks its own claim, refund included, until it undoes that (guarantee L3's caveat; `l3_prefunded_ata_addresses_block_nothing_and_memos_block_only_their_owner`). Nobody else can cause this.
9. **Write-lock contention in the windows (round 2, C7 and C8).** A party that buys more priority per requested compute unit can fill the per-block write budget (24M CU) of the `Launch` account or the pool and keep commits or `execute_batch` out while it pays. The 60-second minimum commit window and the 600-second execution window make the cost linear in time (round-2 estimates: about 0.0035 SOL per 10 s against commits that bid nothing, about 0.012 SOL for the old 30-second execution window); the SDK's compute limits and priority fees raise it further. It is not removed: excluded commits lose the opening price (THREAT_MODEL R23), an excluded batch refunds in full (R7). Creators who want a fair open choose longer windows.
10. **Inside a launch window, the first `launch` to land wins; relayers can delay but not change.** Anyone may open an attested launch inside its window; whoever sees an attestation, a commit or a withdrawal first can hold it back. A held-back attestation past its window's end only burns its leaf. Signed content cannot be changed (guarantees A4, A5). Round 3 (R3-1) showed that delay still mattered: an attestation landed in its window's last seconds, or a window kept free of `launch` by write-lock contention on the `Launch` account, congestion or an outage, left the launch unable ever to go live. Since F1 both are retries: an attestation needs 60 s of its window left when it lands, and after an unused window the creator signs a new one, which replaces the old attestation while the launch is `Configured`. What remains is that contention can keep `launch` out of each window while someone pays for it, and each retry costs the creator a leaf. The LiteSVM harness has no per-account cost tracker, so the contention itself is not reproduced in tests; the tests leave the window unused instead.
11. **Burn-only landings spend leaves.** Any valid signature that cannot attest (no record yet, a window that cannot open, the launch already attested, another identity) lands and spends one of the identity's 1,024 leaves. The client must publish the record and wait for its confirmation (finalized preferred) before attesting, and cap automatic re-signs (SDK 0.4). A record lost in a dropped fork turns its attestation into a burn.
12. **Readers must find the right metadata record.** Anyone can publish junk records under a launch. Readers that resolve the record through `Attestation.metadata_hash` or `Certificate.metadata` are safe; readers that search a launch's records are not (THREAT_MODEL R26).
13. **What wallets show.** By default the mint's uri is empty, so third-party wallets show the name and symbol without the icon. An external uri's content can change. The inline uri is not offered until the wallet and indexer matrix of FACTS (on-chain metadata facts) is run on mainnet.
14. **`MetadataMismatch` (6047) is unreachable with an honest DBC.** Its two LiteSVM mutants are equivalent and survive; host tests and the TLV tests (`the_post_condition_compares_token_2022s_own_encoding`) pin the comparison itself.
15. **Gaps in the tests.**
    - `execute_batch` called through a CPI by another program is tested on the hook's side with a stand-in program at the launchpad's ID (`only_a_buy_inside_execute_batch_passes_the_snipe_shield`); no test drives the real launchpad through a CPI.
    - The hook's rule for a base-vault self-transfer is reachable only by calling the hook directly, because Token-2022 never calls the hook for it (§4).
    - The LiteSVM-only mutation scripts, the host mutation script of the hook and the launchpad and the SDK mutation scripts live outside the repository, kept by the maintainers (`svm-mutants.ps1`, `svm_mutate.py` and `host_mutants.py`, one copy per round since the on-chain metadata stage, each extending the one before; the SDK's `mutants.mjs` and `mutants3.mjs`; SDK 0.5's `qsl_mutants.py`, `c21_removed.py` and `pending_mutants.py`); `tests/integration/mutants.ps1`, `scripts/pq/lms_mutants.py`, `scripts/rules/rules_mutants.py` and `scripts/autopilot/autopilot_mutants.py` are in it.
    - Write-lock contention (round 2's C7 and C8, round 3's R3-1) is argued from Agave's cost model, not reproduced: LiteSVM has no per-account cost tracker.
    - `claim` through the real `execute_batch` is tested in the launchpad's LiteSVM suite; `tests/integration` stands in for it with a transfer out of the exempt batch authority.
    - No full lifecycle has been rehearsed on a Surfpool mainnet fork or on devnet with the final programs; the evidence is LiteSVM against mainnet dumps.
    - `@solana/web3.js` 1.99.x, admitted by the SDK's peer range, is not exercised by any test.
16. **Client work not finished at this revision.** SDK 0.4.0 (the metadata record, message v2, `launch` without metadata, `COMPUTE_UNITS.launch` raised to 300,000, re-sign caps, and round 3's client changes: the new flow status after an unused window, the 60-second window rule, the `MutableOwner` error and destination checks, the fee-recipient refusal) is in `sdk/`, unreleased. SDK 0.5.0 (the QSL-1 checker `checkQsl1` with its badge `qslBadge`, and the guard against older unlanded attestation signatures of item 22) is in `sdk/` too, unreleased and not yet reviewed. The app's adoption of the SDK was in progress when this item was first written. The app-side items then still open from the clients review and round 2: M1 (claim the vault key across tabs before signing: the SDK's vault-key ledger with `navigator.locks`), M3 (the PQ phrase must not be typed into a password field inside a form), L2 (records that can never execute must not block signing), L3 (PQ signing and broadcasting only on a verified cluster), L4 (separate acknowledgments for recipient, mint and off-curve warnings), L5 (the mock must match the live SDK), I1 (the exact batch cap), and round 2's C9 (the auto-execute crank must retry until the window closes). Until the app ships them, an app user relies on the SDK's own refusals. For the programmable launches, SDK 0.6.0 and 0.7.0 are in `sdk/`, unreleased; review 4's client decisions (R2's QSL-1 wording and warnings, R5's freeze payload v2, R6's simulator) are in SDK 0.8.0, unreleased; the app's certificate displays test `certificateChecksComplete` (R4). Audit round 4's client fixes (ID-F1 to ID-F7, C1, C2, the keeper's own observation, never funding recipient accounts, revocation payload v3 and the new autopilot fields; M1 to M3 in the MCP server) are done in SDK 0.9.0, `@noisedlabs/mcp` 0.2.0 and the app (§16). A client still on SDK 0.8 or earlier keeps revocation payload v2, which never matches what the program checks, and the behaviours §16 describes as audited.
17. **Dependencies.** The fixes rely on Token-2022's memo rule (the memo must be the instruction just before the transfer), Token-2022 calling the hook for zero-amount transfers but never for a transfer to the same account, the ATA program's create path, SPL Memo, DBC 0.2.1's swap, fee and heap behaviour (a zero-output buy still makes its token transfer and sets `has_swap`; a buy into the base vault is refused; a buy that completes the curve makes its output transfer before DBC revokes the hook, pinned by audit round 4's `l_i1_…` test; uris up to about 8,685 bytes fit its heap), and Agave's cost model. Token-2022, DBC and DAMM v2 can be upgraded by multisigs; SPL Memo cannot. See SECURITY_GUARANTEES, "Who you still trust", and THREAT_MODEL §6.
18. **The documents are checked by hand.** No automated test compares these documents with the code; this revision was checked against the code and the stage reports listed in each section.
19. **A vault's batch quota can be used up by others (round 3, R3-2).** Anyone may name any PQ vault of the base mint as the token owner of its own commit, so a third party can fill a vault's `PayoutVault` quota before its holder commits; the holder's commit naming it then fails `CapExceeded`. The tokens that commit buys go to the vault. Its refund does not: `commit` fixes `token_owner` and `refund_owner` separately at the first commit (each the committer or a PQ vault of the right mint), and `claim` pays the unspent wSOL to the `refund_owner` the third party chose, normally its own wallet. So the attack costs the part of its commit the batch spent, all of it turned into tokens in the victim's vault. Opening a fresh vault and committing to it in one transaction leaves no ordinary user time to see its address; a leader that reorders transactions still could (not tested). Documented in THREAT_MODEL 5.2.10 and guarantee A7, whose earlier text wrongly said the refund also went to the vault.
20. **Shared accounts share one max-wallet (round 3, R3-5).** During the guard, a holder of about `max_wallet` tokens can fill the Shield's escrow for a mint (blocking every further wrap) or top up someone's canonical ATA (blocking that account's buys and vault `release` payouts) until `guard_end_ts`. Griefing only, at most 72 h (HOOKS.md §9, PRIVACY.md §4).
21. **The wallet-to-wallet lock stops transfers, not changes of control (round 3, R3-4 and the fix review).** It stops token transfers between holders' accounts, and since F2 also consolidation by reassigning a token account's owner: while the guard is on, a transfer other than a sell lands only in an account whose owner can never change. It does not stop control of a position moving with no token transfer at all: a wallet several people control (a multisig, a smart wallet's members, a shared key); a token account owned by a PDA or smart wallet whose own rules hand control to someone else; a PQ vault whose holder signs `withdraw` with `max_amount = 0` and `next_key` set to another party's key (a pure key rotation, which moves no token, so the hook never sees it); and a delegate approval (`Approve`), which gives revocable, shared control and lets the delegate sell during the guard. Guarantee L8, THREAT_MODEL 5.2.5 and 5.2.11, HOOKS.md §9.
22. **An older, unlanded attestation signature can take a freed slot (round 3 fix review, info #2).** Before F1 an attested launch made every other signature of its identity inert. Since F1, once the stored window has passed unused, any valid signature by the identity whose own window still has a minute left attests, including one the creator sent earlier that never landed (a dropped transaction whose bytes an RPC or relayer kept). Whoever holds it can land it first and so pick, among windows and records the creator signed, one that opens up to 7 days later; the creator's new signature then only burns its leaf until that window ends. Every window and record involved was signed by the creator, and the slot becomes free again after it, so the cost is delay. Client rule: land, or let burn, every signature you reveal. SDK 0.5.0 (unreleased) enforces it for the signatures in its leaf ledger: `buildAttestLaunchTx` throws `PendingAttestationError`, before reserving or signing anything, instead of signing a new message for a launch while an earlier recorded signature of it has not landed and its window has not ended (a rebuild of a recorded signature is never refused); `landOrBurnPending` re-sends those signatures and never signs a new one, and with `burnOnly` lands only the ones that would just burn their leaf, which is safe while the current attestation holds the slot; `getLaunchFlowStatus` lists them and warns. A signature that only another device's ledger holds stays invisible until that ledger's records are imported (`importLeafRecords`), and a signature revealed outside any ledger is not covered. Audit round 4 found the guard check-then-act (ID-F4): two concurrent calls for one launch could both pass the check and both sign, and the RPC holding an unlanded signature could switch the guard off by reporting its leaf as used or a clock past its window. A per-launch lock and stricter "pending" rules fix it in SDK 0.9.0 and the app (§16), with a cap on signatures that never land (ID-F2).
23. **Guarded transfers need an immutable-owner destination.** Since F2, while the guard is on, any transfer other than a sell into an account without Token-2022's `ImmutableOwner` extension fails (`MutableOwner`), even with the wallet-to-wallet lock off: buys routed through another program's non-ATA account (some aggregator and multi-hop routes), a vault's `release_to` into such an account, and an unwrap from the Shield into one. Associated token accounts always have the extension, and sells are never affected. Clients route guarded buys straight through DBC into the buyer's ATA.
24. **Creator rules can restrict buys to listed wallets (review 4, R4-2).** A verified rule set can let only the wallets it lists buy from the curve, for up to the whole guard (at most 72 h), a private sale; every holder can still sell. It is disclosed, not prevented: the identity signed the rules, the certificate and verifier show them, and clients explain them in plain English and warn on buys restricted to a key list, rules on wallet-to-wallet transfers and rules active for more than an hour (R2). QSL-1 does not treat a programmable launch as a higher level. Pinned by `insider_only_buying_verifies_and_sells_still_pass` (guarantee CR1, THREAT_MODEL R33).
25. **Vacuous rules carry `RULES_BOUND` (review 4, R4-3).** A rule set that can never refuse (for example DENY when FALSE) verifies, and the certificate then sets the informational bit 4096. The bit means "rules are bound", not "holders are protected"; clients warn on vacuous rules (R2). Pinned by `vacuous_rules_verify_and_never_refuse` (THREAT_MODEL R34).
26. **Holders' exits skip creator rules, so rules can be routed around (review 4, R1).** A transfer whose top-level instruction is the Noised PQ vault or the Shield never meets the creator's rules; the built-in checks (the lock, the caps, `MutableOwner`) still apply to it. So wrapping, moving zTokens (which have no hook) and unwrapping passes rules that restrict wallet-to-wallet transfers or owner lists, but never the built-in lock. A call into the vault or the Shield made by another program through CPI has that program as its top-level instruction and stays subject to the rules (guarantee CR3, THREAT_MODEL R35, PRIVACY.md §4).
27. **The autopilot's price bound has residuals.** A keeper that sandwiches a buyback gains at most about premium × amount against the bound; a party that makes every observation of a 15-to-30-minute window can walk the reference up at most 5% per hour (paying the pool's fees both ways) or, after a market fall nobody observed, price a buyback at the window's lowest price; dumping the pool in an observation every 15 minutes holds buybacks back, and since the F1 fix of the framework-free rewrite's review the same round trip (a trade across the threshold, `update_price`, the trade back) holds back any step whose condition reads the price, burns and payouts included, for about 96 × 2 × fee × the volume that crosses the threshold per day (liveness only: the treasury keeps its funds; §17); after a genuine rally buybacks pause until the reference catches up. One honest observation per window closes the first two: every keeper run is one, and `update_price` is free (SPEC §9, THREAT_MODEL R36, R37). **The bound depends on an honest observation in each window** (audit round 4, A4-1): after a fall nobody observed, a keeper-attacker whose pumped observation, made a minute before a daily node is due, is the only one in the window prices the buyback at the stale reference; the SDK keeper skipped its own observation when one was already counting, and so lost that race. Keepers now make their own observation at least 60 s before every buyback (SDK 0.9.0), and whoever runs keepers should run an independent `update_price` at least every 10 minutes, so that every 15-minute bucket holds an honest observation.
28. **Withheld authorizations.** Once a `pq_authorize` authorization has landed, anyone may use it while it is still valid: an activation only as its signed `activate_at` and the 72-hour timelock allow, a freeze only until its signed `not_after` (review 4, R5; before it, a landed freeze could be used at any later time), a revocation at any time (it only removes power). An authorization that was signed but has not landed can be held back by whoever relays it, and authorizations are ordered by landing slot, not by signing time. Clients land their own through several RPCs (THREAT_MODEL R38).
29. **Automation needs callers, and its operator and keepers are classical keys.** Nothing runs unless someone sends `run`, `apply_pending`, `update_price` and `bind_pool`; keepers are paid by bounded tips (a node whose per-run cap is under 0.001 SOL pays less than a full tip; a keeper's partial buyback earns only its share of a tip, audit round 4, A4-2; a burn before graduation earns nothing, A4-4, so such a node runs only if someone runs it unpaid). Keepers never pay for recipients' accounts: a distribution skips a recipient without one (M4, item 31). An operator (for example an AI agent) is an Ed25519 key: forged or hijacked, it can choose only the timing and amount of its own nodes within the on-chain bounds, until the identity revokes it (THREAT_MODEL R39, R40).
30. **`bind_pool` trusts two Meteora behaviours.** It accepts the pool of any DAMM v2 config whose pool creator authority is DBC's pool authority, relying on DAMM v2 letting only that authority create a pool under such a config (pinned by a LiteSVM test for static configs; dynamic configs require the authority as a signer per the IDL, not separately tested) and on DBC signing as that authority only in migration (THREAT_MODEL R41).
31. **Autopilot governance and distributions since audit round 4.** A revocation names the activation it revokes (payload v3, A4-3): after any re-activation is applied, even of the same graph, a revocation signed earlier can no longer be used, and the identity signs a new one. A distribution pays only recipients whose canonical wSOL ATA holds an initialized wSOL account they own (M4); the others are skipped, their share stays in the treasury and still counts against the node's budget, a reassigned ATA is skipped too, and a run that would pay nobody fails (`NoRecipientAccount`). So a recipient that wants its share keeps its wSOL account open. A graph that names a PQ vault of another mint as a recipient loses that share once anyone creates the vault's wSOL ATA; clients refuse such recipients (round-4 C1: SDK 0.9.0's `checkGraphRecipients` and the app's autopilot editor).
32. **Signing clients trust the device's clock** (audit round 4, ID-F5). Signed times (an attestation's window, an activation's `activate_at`, a freeze's `not_after`) come from the cluster's clock. As audited the SDK read it from the RPC alone, so a lying RPC could turn a one-hour freeze approval into one usable for a year. The fix (SDK 0.9.0 and the app) refuses to sign when the RPC's clock and the device's differ by more than 120 s (and, when a second RPC is given, that one's), shows absolute dates and caps `not_after` at 24 hours; a device whose own clock is wrong together with an RPC that agrees with it is not caught.
33. **Autopilot price conditions are `u64`** (audit round 4, L-i3). `PRICE_LTE` and `PRICE_GTE` compare the reference sqrt price with a `u64`, so they can only name a price below one lamport per raw token unit (0.001 SOL per whole token at 6 decimals, 1 SOL at 9); the SDK refuses a higher one (`PriceOutOfRange`).

## 7. What these reviews are not

- **They are not an independent audit.** The README's "pre-audit" status stands, and [OPERATIONS.md](OPERATIONS.md) requires at least two independent audits before the programs are finalized.
- **The evidence is local.** It comes from LiteSVM runs against mainnet program dumps, host tests and independent Python re-derivations, not from a live cluster.
- **The app has not been reviewed as a whole since the clients review**, apart from the SDK and UI review of the programmable staging module (its SDK findings are fixed in SDK 0.6.0, [sdk/CHANGELOG.md](../sdk/CHANGELOG.md)) and audit round 4's custody and identity-signing lenses, which looked at its autopilot editor and signing paths (§16). It has since adopted SDK 0.9.0 (`app/test/sdk09-adoption.test.ts`); that adoption has not been reviewed separately.
- **Silence is not proof.** A lens that reported nothing in an area does not prove the area is free of bugs.

## 9. PQ v2: spec review, adversarial review and fix (2026-10-09)

PQ v2 made every launch post-quantum attested, folded in the round-2 program fixes, and moved payouts, fees and the treasury into PQ custody. Three reviews shaped it.

- "Two adversarial audit rounds ... small audited code ... all four programs finalized" were not facts: AI-agent reviews are not an audit, and nothing is finalized yet.

## 11. On-chain metadata review and fix (2026-10-09)

The change stores a token's metadata on-chain (`TokenMeta`, `publish_metadata`), binds it into the attestation (message v2), and makes `launch()` take no arguments and certify the mint's metadata (bit `METADATA_ONCHAIN`). One reviewer checked it against its design, re-derived every vector in independent Python, and ran the largest records through migration to DAMM v2. 2 findings:

- **Medium: a relayer could turn an attesting signature into a burn, repeatedly.** `attest_launch`'s `token_meta` account was unconstrained, so whoever landed the creator's signature first could name any other account: the signature then only burned its leaf, the creator's own transaction failed with `PqLeafUsed`, and every re-sign could be treated the same way, so the launch never went live (proof of concept: four rounds burned four leaves). **Fix:** the handler derives `["metadata", launch, metadata_hash]` itself and fails the transaction with `ConstraintSeeds` for any other account, burning nothing; the account stays unchecked in content so a valid signature still lands whatever is at the right address. Regression tests: `a_relayer_cannot_turn_an_attesting_signature_into_a_burn` (six decoys refused, then the creator's own transaction attests and the launch goes live) and `attest_launch_refuses_any_account_but_the_one_its_hash_names`; new mutants (integration L74, LiteSVM `attest-takes-any-record-account`), each killed only by its new test.
- **Info: the documents lagged the code.** Fixed in part by the metadata stage (SPEC §1, guarantees T3, THREAT_MODEL, FACTS, PRIVACY, ARCHITECTURE) and in full by this revision.

Checked and found sound: the record layout and rules, metadata hash v2 and message v2, the content-addressed record (no other bytes can sit at an attested address), type and version confusion, Anchor's `init_if_needed` for pre-funded addresses, the post-condition against Token-2022's own encoding, the base64 encoder, byte parity of every vector, and liveness through migration at the largest records.

**Final verification** (2026-10-09, [verification summary](evidence/2026-10-09-metadata-final-verification.md), overall PASS): SBPF v3 builds of all four programs without warnings; fmt and clippy clean in four workspaces; host 151, known answers 35, integration 98, launchpad LiteSVM 59, shield 17; the three Python reference checks; 148 of 148 integration mutants (38 vault, 36 hook, 74 launchpad) and 23 of 23 LiteSVM-only mutants killed, the 2 equivalent `MetadataMismatch` mutants surviving as documented (§6 item 14).

**R3-1, medium: a missed window bricked the launch.** `attest_launch` wrote the attestation only while it was empty, and nothing ever replaced it; `launch` runs only inside the stored window. So if no `launch` landed by `not_after`, every later signature by the identity only burned a leaf and the launch stayed `Configured` forever. Ways to get there: fill the `Launch` account's per-block write budget for the window (it is the one writable account of `launch` that does not derive from the fresh mint), congestion or a keeper outage, or a relayer holding the creator's attestation and landing it at `now == not_after`, which `launch_window_is_valid` accepted. Proof of concept: four re-signed windows burned four leaves and the stored window never changed; `launch` failed `NotLaunchWindow` in every new window and 30 days later; 35,308,080 lamports of rent stayed locked with a small record (LiteSVM's rent rate); the relayer and late-landing variants were reproduced too. The skeptics rated it low: no user funds exist before `launch`, the extra harm over the documented contention residual is the creator's rent and work, and the SDK already described the permanence as expected. **F1 (fixed, merged 2026-10-10):**
- `attest_launch` attests a valid signature by the launch's identity when the attestation slot is free: `slot_free = attestation.launch == Pubkey::default() || (launch.status == Configured && now > attestation.not_after)`, besides the existing conditions (the identity, the record at its canonical address, a valid window). A replacement writes every field (version, launch, identity, leaf, metadata hash, message hash, slot, both window ends, and the 2,508-byte signature, which always fills the account exactly, so no old byte survives; the bump is rewritten with the same value) and emits `LaunchAttested` again with its unchanged layout. Every valid signature still burns its leaf first. An attestation whose window has not ended (before it, inside it, or in its last second) and any attestation of a `Live` launch are never replaced; such a signature only burns its leaf (`PqLeafConsumed`). The certificate keeps the attestation that opened the launch (the code path is unchanged). No account, event or instruction layout changed;
- `launch_window_is_valid` also requires `max(now, not_before) + MIN_LAUNCH_WINDOW_SECS <= not_after`: an attestation needs at least 60 s of its window left when it lands, otherwise it only burns its leaf. `launch` needs `now <= not_after` and a replacement `now > not_after`, so within one slot both never apply;

Readers must not cache the attestation before `Live`: `LaunchAttested` can be emitted more than once per launch, the latest is authoritative while the launch is `Configured`, and once `Live` the attestation is the one whose leaf and slot the certificate records;

**R3-2, low: payout-vault quota griefing.** A commit may name any PQ vault of the base mint as its token owner (`pq::is_payout_owner` checks owner program, discriminator, version and mint, not who controls the vault), and every commit naming a vault counts against that vault's one `PayoutVault` total. ### Launch lens: no exploitable issue

The launch lens found no exploitable issue in the launchpad or the hook and raised three info items.

- **L-i1: DBC's order inside a curve-completing buy.** In DBC 0.2.1 a buy that completes the curve makes its output transfer (`process_swap.rs`, lines 302 to 310), which runs the hook, before DBC revokes the hook in the same swap (around lines 366 and 370). So the hook still sees such a buy and, during the snipe shield, refuses it. Were the order reversed, a buy completing the curve during the shield would pass unchecked. New LiteSVM test `l_i1_a_buy_completing_the_curve_during_the_shield_is_refused` (`programs/noised-launchpad/tests/litesvm/tests/audit_round4.rs`): on a launch without buy or wallet caps and without a batch, a buy large enough to complete the curve, sent in the shield's last second, fails `SnipeShield` and changes nothing (the pool's bytes, the mint's hook, the buyer's balance); the same buy a second later completes the curve, and DBC revokes the hook inside it. The test pins behaviour that is already right and fails if a DBC release changes the order; the dependency is added to §6 items 5 and 17.
- **L-i2: no commit after the batch.** `commit` now requires `!launch.executed` (`AlreadyExecuted`, 6025, an existing error). It is unreachable today, because the batch executes only after the commit window closes. `l_i2_no_commit_after_the_batch` marks a launch executed inside its commit window and checks that a further commit and a first commit both fail and change nothing, while the same commit on the unmarked launch goes through.
- **L-i3: price conditions are `u64`.** `PRICE_LTE` and `PRICE_GTE` (opcodes 0x23 and 0x24) compare the reference sqrt price (Q64.64, a `u128`) with a `u64` argument, so a condition can only name a sqrt price below 2^64: a price below one lamport per raw token unit (0.001 SOL per whole token at 6 decimals, 1 SOL at 9). Known; the SDK refuses a price it cannot express (`PriceOutOfRange`). Documented only (SPEC §9.2).

### Autopilot lens

**A4-1: the honest-observation dependency (a documented residual, shown concretely).** The documented bound (§6 item 27, THREAT_MODEL R36) says that a party making every observation of a window can, after a fall nobody observed, price a buyback at the window's lowest price. The lens showed how that plays out with the daily buyback template and the SDK keeper. During a day the pool fell to 0.718 of the reference and nobody called `update_price` (a daily node's only observations are its keepers'). A minute before the node was due, the attacker made one pumped observation (buy, `update_price`, sell back, in one transaction, at a cost of 0.043 SOL). Every observation in the window was then its own, so the bound was the stale reference (raised by the hour's rise allowance), not the fallen market. The SDK keeper (`sdk/src/autopilot/keeper.ts`) sends `update_price` only when no observation is counting; at the due time the attacker's already spanned a minute, so the keeper went straight to `run` and lost the race to the attacker's own run inside a pump. With one honest observation of the fall, the full buyback could not run at all (the test's counterfactual). In the reviewer's recorded run the final sandwiched buyback was refused by DAMM v2's own slippage check (`ExceededSlippage`), so the round has no measured figure for the attacker's gain; the mechanism is the documented one. **Decision, no program change:** a keeper always makes its own `update_price` observation at least 60 s before a buyback, even when one is already counting (done in SDK 0.9.0: `planKeeperStep` asks for the keeper's own `update_price` and runs the buyback only a minute after it, `ownObservationAt`); whoever runs keepers should also run an independent observer at least every 10 minutes, so that every 15-minute bucket holds an honest observation; the dependency is stated in §6 item 27, SPEC §9.6, SECURITY_GUARANTEES AP5, THREAT_MODEL R36 and OPERATIONS (Keepers).

**A4-2, low-medium: a minimal keeper buyback throttled the daily run.** As audited, a keeper-chosen amount counted as the node's run. With the daily template (`EVERY` 1 day, one run per epoch, a quarter SOL a day), a griefing keeper ran the node as soon as it was due for the least it may choose (1% of the pool's SOL, about 0.1 SOL), was paid the full tip, and the honest keeper's full run was refused (`NodeNotDue`) for the rest of the day. Over three days the node bought back 302,403,980 of the promised 750,000,000 lamports, and the griefer earned 300,000 lamports in tips. **Fix:** a keeper's buyback for less than the node's run cap is a *partial* run. The cap is `min(p1 - partial_spent[node], budget left this epoch)`. A partial run spends from the budget, adds its amount to `partial_spent[node]` and earns at most `TIP_LAMPORTS × amount / p1`, but does not set `last_run` and does not count toward `max_runs_per_epoch`, so the rest of the run stays open. A chosen amount must equal the cap or leave at least the floor (1% of the pool's SOL) for the rest (`RemainderTooSmall`, 6041), so no partial run leaves a remainder too small to swap. Because the cap shrinks by what the open run's parts moved, the parts never move more than the run would, even across epochs; a completed run and a newly applied graph clear `partial_spent`. A keeper that names no amount moves everything the treasury allows, and that counts as the run, as before; the operator's amounts on its own nodes are never partial. Tests: `a4_2_a_partial_buyback_does_not_use_up_the_daily_run` (the proof of concept inverted: the honest keeper runs the rest the same day, three days buy back the whole 750,000,000 lamports, and the griefer's tips total 122,760), `a4_2_partial_runs_never_move_more_than_the_run` (a once-only node split over three days moves its `p1` once, then never runs again), `a4_2_a_new_graph_starts_with_no_partial_runs`; host `a_keepers_partial_buyback_leaves_the_run_open`, `a_partial_buyback_is_recorded_as_no_run`, `a_partial_buyback_earns_its_share_of_a_tip`. 18 mutants.

**A4-3, low: a re-appointed operator could not be revoked again.** Revocation payload v2 was `SHA256("PHX-OP-REVOKE-V2" ‖ autopilot ‖ graph_hash ‖ operator)`. A creator that revoked its operator as a precaution and then appointed it again by re-activating the same graph (after the 72-hour timelock) could never revoke it again: a new signature over the revocation had the same payload, so `pq_authorize` found the `Authorization` already written and only burned the leaf, and the existing authorization was the latest revocation used, so `revoke_operator` refused it (`StaleAuthorization`). Only a different graph, 72 hours later, took the operator's power away; meanwhile it kept running its gated node. **Fix (A4-3 with A4-S):** the autopilot stores the running graph's activation, `active_auth` (the address of the `Authorization` that activated it) and `active_activate_at` (the `activate_at` it signed), written by `apply_pending` and changed only when another graph is applied. Revocation payload v3 is `SHA256("PHX-OP-REVOKE-V3" ‖ autopilot ‖ active_graph ‖ operator ‖ active_auth)`. A revocation is therefore bound to one activation: an operator appointed again, even by the same graph, needs and can always get a new revocation, and a revocation signed for an earlier activation cannot reach it (once a re-activation is applied, it is dead and must be signed again). With `active_graph`, the two fields also let anyone re-verify the running graph's LMS authorization from account state: `SHA256("PHX-GRAPH-ACT-V1" ‖ autopilot ‖ active_graph ‖ i64le(active_activate_at))` is the payload, the `Authorization` at `["auth", launch, [1], payload]` must be `active_auth`, and it holds the signature (QSL-1's programmable checks). Tests: `a4_3_a_reappointed_operator_is_revoked_again` (the proof of concept inverted), `a4_s_the_running_graphs_activation_is_kept` (a queued replacement, a revocation and a freeze leave it unchanged); host `payload_hashes_follow_the_documented_messages`, `autopilot_layout_appends_the_audit_4_fields`. 6 mutants.

**A4-4, low: burns before graduation earned more in tips than they cost.** Before graduation the reference is the launch's migration price (in the reviewer's launch, 25 times the curve's opening price), and a burn's tip was 10% of the burned tokens' value at the reference. Tokens bought early on the curve and sent to the treasury before each run of a 10-minute burn node therefore earned more in tips than they cost: 50 burns, each of tokens that had cost the attacker 48,404 lamports, took the day's 5,000,000-lamport tip cap, a net gain of 2,579,800 lamports a day per autopilot. **Fix** (`logic::burn_price`): before graduation a burn is valued at nothing, so it earns no tip; after graduation its tokens are valued at the lower of the reference and the lowest price observed in the window, this run's own observation included (`reference::lowest`, which needs no minimum span). Tests: `a4_4_burns_earn_no_tips_before_graduation` (60 burns, no tip), `a4_4_burns_after_graduation_are_valued_at_the_lowest_observed_price` (after a 28.2% fall nobody observed, a burn worth a full tip at the reference earned 71,818 lamports); host `burns_are_valued_at_nothing_before_graduation_then_at_the_lowest_price`, `the_lowest_price_of_the_window_needs_no_span`. 9 mutants. Consequence: before graduation a burn node pays its callers nothing, so it runs only if someone runs it unpaid (§6 item 29).

### Custody lens

The vault, the Shield and `noised-lmots` on-chain: clean. The findings concern the autopilot's keeper economics (M4, fixed in the program) and client code.

**M4, medium (keepers): recipients made keepers pay rent.** A `DISTRIBUTE_SOL` run required every recipient's canonical wSOL account to exist, so the SDK keeper created missing ones. Recipients that closed their wSOL accounts after each payout (taking the rent back), or never had one, made the keeper pay a token account's rent per recipient per run (2,039,280 lamports in LiteSVM; 1,488,440 at mainnet's current rate) for a tip of at most 100,000. PoC `audit4_custody_keeper.test.ts`. **Fix (program):** the caller still passes every recipient's canonical wSOL ATA address, writable, in graph order (`InvalidRecipientAccounts` otherwise), but a recipient whose address does not hold an initialized SPL Token wSOL account owned by that recipient is skipped, and its share stays in the treasury; a run that would pay nobody fails `NoRecipientAccount` (6042). The skipped share is still counted against the node's budget (`NodeRan.spent` is the planned total; what was paid is `spent - kept`), and the tip is a share of what was paid. `NodeRan` gains `skipped` (bit i set: recipient i skipped) and `kept`. A canonical wSOL ATA whose owner field was reassigned is now skipped too, where before it was paid. Keepers never create recipient accounts (client side, with stated costs: done in SDK 0.9.0, whose `buildRunTx` the MCP server's `build_run_tx` uses). Tests: `m4_recipients_without_accounts_are_skipped_and_cost_keepers_nothing` (the proof of concept ported: three recipients that close their accounts; the run creates nothing and the keeper pays only its fee; 51,891 CU with one of three skipped), `m4_a_share_of_nothing_is_no_skip`, and `distribute_pays_only_recipients_with_an_account` (formerly `distribute_needs_every_recipient_account`). 12 mutants, 3 of them documented equivalents (a recipient account of another program, of another mint, or frozen: the ATA program and wSOL rule each out).

**C1, low-medium: graph recipients were not checked.** Round 3's R3-3 for fee recipients, in autopilot graphs: a PQ vault of another mint named as a `DISTRIBUTE_SOL` recipient can never move the wSOL paid to its ATA, so its share is lost as soon as that account exists (since M4 a recipient without the account is skipped, but anyone can create it). PoC `poc_custody_autopilot_recipient.test.ts`. Decision: the SDK's `planGraphActivation` and `buildPublishGraphTx` and the app's autopilot editor check graph recipients as fee recipients are checked (`checkFeeRecipients`, wSOL): refuse a PQ vault of another mint, warn about other program-owned recipients. Fixed in SDK 0.9.0 (`checkGraphRecipients`, run by `planGraphActivation`, `buildPublishGraphTx` and `buildPublishGraphV1Tx`) and in the app's autopilot editor (its payee check).

**C2, low: the pending-withdrawal search read spam first.** `findPendingWithdrawal` reads a vault's history newest first. Successful spam transactions that name the vault and its current payout address are read before a real withdrawal, so the search can run out of its budget: a delay on the device that holds the ledger, and `IncompleteSearchError` (signing refused, by design) on a second device. Nothing is ever signed twice. PoC `poc_custody_search_spam.test.ts`. Decision: read transactions with a withdraw-shaped instruction (the vault program, the withdraw discriminator, this vault) first, and correct the SDK's doc comment that claimed otherwise. Fixed in SDK 0.9.0.

**M1 to M3: untrusted text in the MCP server.** M1 (medium): `verify_token` returned `documentProblems` (creator text of up to about 3 KB, newlines and bidi controls included) and `links[].host` unmarked, so a creator could write instructions an agent might follow. M2 (low-medium): `cleanText` missed invisible characters (Unicode tags U+E0000 to U+E007F, variation selectors U+FE00 to U+FE0F and U+E0100 to U+E01EF, U+034F, U+115F, U+3164, U+17B4). M3 (low): RPC-provided text (`simulation.error`, exception messages) was neither wrapped nor cleaned. PoC `audit4_custody.test.ts` (now inverted into the regression tests of `packages/noised-mcp/test/audit4_custody.test.ts`). Decision: every creator and RPC string is wrapped with `untrusted()`; `cleanText` strips `\p{Cf}`, `\p{Co}`, `\p{Cn}` and those characters (or keeps to an allow-list); the `localnet` cluster accepts plain http only to a loopback host (an info item). Fixed in `@noisedlabs/mcp` 0.2.0. A second info item: the SDK keeper's sleep reads the RPC's clock.

### Identity-signing lens

**ID-F1, medium-high: a dishonest RPC could steer a second device onto a withheld signature's leaf.** As audited, the client chose a leaf by starting at a public probe, `u16be(SHA256("phalanx/pq/identity/leaf" ‖ I ‖ message)[0..2]) mod 1024`, and stepping past every leaf the RPC's bitmap reported as used and every leaf in its own ledger. Two devices that share an identity (a synced passkey on a second device, or one device whose site data was cleared after a dropped send) do not see each other's unlanded signatures. An RPC or relayer that kept one device's signature without landing it could then report fake used bits to the other device so that its probe walked onto exactly that leaf: the identity signed two different messages at one one-time leaf, which lets whoever holds both forge further signatures at it (POST_QUANTUM.md §3.5). §6 item 2 had put the two-device collision at about 1 in 1,024 per pair of messages; against a dishonest RPC it was certain. The same RPC's clock also decided the signed times (ID-F5). Two independent reports, each with a proof of concept against the SDK: the identity-signing lens (`poc_identity_steer.test.ts`: fake bits from the probe's start up to the target leaf) and the custody lens's helper (its own `poc_identity_steer.test.ts`: every leaf but the target reported as used, which needs no knowledge of the second device's message; both messages verify at the one leaf; a second test reaches the same leaf with no lying RPC, through a tampered "other device" export, ID-F2b). **Decision, client only** (the program never checks how a leaf was chosen):
- the probe's start becomes secret and keyed: `u16be(HMAC-SHA256(key = a probe key derived from the identity's SEED, "phalanx/pq/identity/leaf/v2" ‖ message)[0..2]) mod 1024`. The same message still gets the same leaf, so a retry stays idempotent, but nobody without the identity's secret can predict or steer it; vectors and tests are updated;
- the leaf is chosen from the ledger and the in-process guard only; bits reported by the RPC never move it;
- before reserving, the client refuses (it does not just warn) if the chosen leaf is set on-chain, or if the bitmap shows any used leaf this ledger has no record of ("this key was used elsewhere: import that device's records first"); the app blocks signing there, offers to import the other device's records or to confirm that the identity is fresh, and shows `built.warnings` everywhere;
- before every signature that contains a time, the cluster's clock is compared with the device's clock (and with a second RPC when one is available); more than 120 s apart, the client refuses with a plain message;
- the key screens say it plainly (the app: "Use one device per quantum key unless you import its records.").

**Done** in SDK 0.9.0 and in the app. The SDK: the secret, keyed probe (`LmsKeyPair.leafProbeStart`), `UnknownLeavesError` and `acknowledgeUnknownLeaves`, and `checkClockSkew` / `ClockSkewError` before every signature that names a time. The app: a "your quantum key was used somewhere else" panel (`app/src/components/KeyUsedElsewhere.tsx`, wording in `app/src/lib/signErrors.ts`) that offers to import the other device's records and, only for a count of at most 3 and after reading the key's record again (on a second network connection when the build has one), a typed confirmation; the clock refusal in plain words; the cross-tab signing lock (`navigator.locks`); the cap on unlanded signatures; and the key screen's line "Use one device per quantum key unless you import its records." What remains is §6 item 2: two devices that do not sync can each sign an unlanded message at the same leaf with odds of about 1 in 1,024 per pair of messages, and nobody can steer that.

**ID-F2, low-medium: no cap on signatures that never land.** Every signature that does not land leaves a leaf whoever holds it can use (§6 item 22). Decision: at most 3 attestation signatures per launch and 3 `pq_authorize` signatures per launch and kind, per ledger, without an explicit "I understand" confirmation, counting unlanded signatures and not only burn-only landings; the app's automatic re-send loops never create a new signature without user action. Fixed in SDK 0.9.0 (`SignatureCapError`, `MAX_UNLANDED_SIGNATURES`, `acknowledgeExtraSignatures`) and in the app (an explicit confirmation).

**ID-F3 (low) and ID-F2b: an import could re-point a message.** `importLeafRecords` accepted records without a signature (which cannot be authenticated) and let them point a message at another leaf, so a tampered "other device" export with one unsigned record made a rebuild of an already signed message sign again at another device's leaf (the second steer test). Decision: an unsigned record imports only as "leaf taken" (burned), never assigning a message, and an import never re-points the leaf of a message that holds a live record. Fixed in SDK 0.9.0.

**ID-F4: the pending-signature guard was check-then-act.** SDK 0.5's guard (`PendingAttestationError`, §6 item 22) checked for a pending signature, then reserved and signed. Two concurrent calls for one launch with different windows (a double click after editing the opening time, two tabs on an IndexedDB ledger, a retry loop) both passed the check and both signed, and the RPC holding an unlanded signature could turn the guard off by reporting that signature's leaf as used, or a clock past its window. PoC `poc_identity_pending_race.test.ts` (3 tests). Decision: a per-launch lock (Web Locks in the app, an async mutex in the SDK) around the check, the reservation and the signature in `buildAttestLaunchTx` and `buildPqAuthorizeTx`; an older signature stays pending unless its leaf landed with a known outcome. Fixed in SDK 0.9.0 (`signingLock`, `signingLockName`) and in the app (`navigator.locks`).

**ID-F5: signed times came from the RPC's clock.** Windows, `activate_at` and a freeze's `not_after` were computed from the RPC's `Clock` alone (the SDK refused only absurd values). An RPC whose clock ran a year ahead turned the default one-hour freeze approval into one usable for a year on the real chain, while every line said "about 1 hour": the withheld freeze review 4's R5 set out to bound. PoC `poc_identity_freeze_clock.test.ts`. Decision: the clock cross-check of ID-F1 (refuse beyond 120 s); absolute dates shown for windows, `activate_at` and `not_after`; a freeze's `not_after` at most 24 h after the device's clock, and an attestation window at most the program's 7-day lead. Fixed in SDK 0.9.0 (`ClockSkewError`, `FREEZE_MAX_VALID_SECS`, the absolute `…Iso` dates) and in the app (UTC dates; its freeze approval stops at 24 hours).

**ID-F6: the plan was checked after signing.** `buildPlannedAttestTx` compared the signed message with the plan's reviewed message only after the identity had signed and the ledger had recorded it. A stale or edited plan therefore produced a recorded, landable signature over an unreviewed message, which then blocked new windows and was broadcast by `landOrBurnPending`. PoC `poc_identity_postcheck.test.ts`. Decision: compare `plan.messageHash` before reserving (it is passed into `buildAttestLaunchTx`), and read `identity.keyPair` once on entry in the attestation and authorization builders. Fixed in SDK 0.9.0.

**ID-F7: zeroization.** Best effort: wipe the concatenation buffer in `lmots.ts`'s secret derivation and the chain-walk buffers, and hash the master concatenation in `keys.ts` through `hashWiped`. Done in SDK 0.9.0 (signatures unchanged byte for byte).

A further proof of concept, `poc_identity_forge_odds.test.ts`, measures how cheap a forgery becomes once one leaf has signed two real attestation messages. Info items: the app's freeze code must build freeze payload v2 (the app polish round covers it once the app uses SDK 0.8), and the demo `signAuth` path should go through `buildPqAuthorizeTx`'s checks too.

### Accepted residuals after round 4

- **The honest-observation dependency** (A4-1): a buyback's bound is only as good as the observations in its window. Keepers observe before every buyback, and an independent observer every 10 minutes or less closes the gap (§6 item 27).
- **Partial buybacks** (A4-2): a keeper may still split a node's run into parts of at least 1% of the pool's SOL; together they move at most the run and earn at most one tip.
- **A revocation names one activation** (A4-3): after any re-activation is applied, even of the same graph, an earlier revocation is dead and the identity signs a new one (§6 item 31).
- **Burns before graduation pay nothing** (A4-4): such nodes run only if someone runs them unpaid (§6 item 29).
- **Distributions skip recipients without a wSOL account** (M4): such a recipient gets nothing from that run, its share stays in the treasury but counts against the node's budget, and a run that would pay nobody fails (§6 item 31).
- **One device per quantum key, unless you import its records** (ID-F1, best practice): with the fix, an unsynced second device still collides with an unlanded signature at about 1 in 1,024 per pair of messages, but nobody can steer it. The clock check compares the cluster with the device's own clock, so a device with a wrong clock and an RPC that agrees with it are not caught (§6 items 2 and 32).
- **Spam slows the pending-withdrawal search** (C2): a delay at most; a search that cannot finish refuses to sign.
- **Untrusted text stays text an agent reads** (M1 to M3): marking it untrusted and stripping invisible characters does not make a model ignore it.
- **Price conditions are `u64`** (L-i3, §6 item 33).
- **Older clients.** A client built on SDK 0.8 or earlier, or a copy of the app from before its SDK 0.9.0 adoption, still behaves as each finding above describes: use SDK 0.9.0 or later.
